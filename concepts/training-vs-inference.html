Training vs. Inference

"Training an AI" and "using an AI" are computationally almost opposite activities — one happens once and is brutally expensive, the other happens constantly and needs to be fast and cheap.

01 The plain-English version

Training is the process of teaching a model — feeding it data over and over, checking its mistakes, and adjusting its internal numbers until it gets good at the task. This can take anywhere from minutes to months and often costs serious money in compute.

Inference is everything that happens after: using the already-trained model to make a prediction on new data, one time, quickly. When you ask a chatbot a question, you're triggering inference, not training — the model's weights don't change from talking to you.

02 The formal version

During training, the model repeatedly performs a forward pass (compute a prediction), measures the loss (how wrong that prediction was), and performs a backward pass (adjust weights to reduce that loss) — see backpropagation. This loop runs over the training dataset for many epochs, and requires storing gradients and often running on specialized hardware (GPUs/TPUs) in parallel.

During inference, only the forward pass runs — the model's weights are frozen. No gradients are computed and no data is stored for correction. This makes inference dramatically cheaper per request than training, though at massive scale (millions of users) inference cost in aggregate can still exceed training cost.

03 The flow

Training happens once (or periodically); inference happens every single time someone uses the model.

04 Worked example

A concrete cost comparison

Training a large language model from scratch can involve processing trillions of tokens across thousands of GPUs running for weeks — costs that can reach tens of millions of dollars for frontier models. A single inference request — you typing a question and getting an answer — might take under a second and cost a small fraction of a cent. The asymmetry is why companies train a model once and then serve it to millions of users: the enormous training cost gets amortized across an enormous number of cheap inferences.

05 Where this shows up

Real-world use

This split explains a lot of AI product design: a company might retrain (or fine-tune) its model monthly while serving inference continuously, 24/7, at global scale. It's also why "the model learned something from our conversation" is usually false for most consumer chatbots — nothing is being trained in real time; the illusion of memory comes from re-feeding the conversation history back in at each inference step.

06 Common misconceptions

✕

Talking to a chatbot trains it on your conversation in real time. Typically false — inference doesn't update weights. Any "memory" comes from re-supplying context, or from a separate, later training/fine-tuning process the company runs deliberately.

✕

Inference is trivial and doesn't need optimization. At scale, inference cost and latency are major engineering problems — this is what techniques like quantization and model distillation exist to address.

✓

Correct framing: training builds the model's knowledge; inference is spending that knowledge, one query at a time, without changing it.
