Applied AI · Concept 02

Recommendation Systems

Quietly one of the highest-revenue-impact applications of ML in existence — the algorithm deciding your next video, product, or song is doing real, substantial machine learning.

01 The plain-English version

Collaborative filtering recommends things based on what similar users liked — "people who enjoyed what you enjoyed also liked this" — without needing to understand anything about the content itself, purely from patterns in user behavior.

Content-based filtering instead looks at the properties of items you've liked before — genre, actors, audio features — and recommends similar items, which works even for brand-new items with no user interaction history yet.

02 The formal version

Classical collaborative filtering uses matrix factorization: given a sparse matrix of users × items × ratings, decompose it into two smaller matrices — user embeddings and item embeddings — such that their product approximates known ratings; the same embeddings can then predict ratings for items a user hasn't interacted with yet, and dot-product similarity between embeddings finds "similar" users or items.

Modern production systems are typically hybrid and deep-learning-based: a two-stage pipeline first uses a lightweight model to retrieve a few hundred plausible candidates from millions of items (candidate generation), then a more expensive, more accurate deep model reranks that shortlist using dozens or hundreds of features — recency, session context, predicted watch-time — a balance between speed and precision that pure matrix factorization alone can't achieve at scale.

03 The flow

Production recommenders narrow millions of items down to a shortlist cheaply, then spend more computation ranking just that shortlist precisely.

04 Worked example

Matrix factorization, simplified

If a user's learned embedding is [0.8, -0.2, 0.5] (representing latent preferences like "likes thrillers," "dislikes slow pacing," "likes strong female leads") and a movie's embedding is [0.7, -0.4, 0.6], the dot product (0.8×0.7) + (-0.2×-0.4) + (0.5×0.6) = 0.56 + 0.08 + 0.3 = 0.94 predicts a high rating — even though no one hand-labeled the movie as "a thriller" for this system; the dimensions emerged purely from patterns in rating data.

05 Where this shows up

Real-world use

This is the technology behind Netflix's "Because you watched...", Amazon's "customers also bought," Spotify's Discover Weekly, and the feed-ranking algorithms behind most social media platforms — arguably some of the most economically significant deployed ML systems in existence, given how directly they drive engagement and revenue.

06 Common misconceptions

✕

Recommendation systems just find what's objectively "best" for you. Most production systems are optimized for engagement or predicted revenue, not necessarily long-term user wellbeing — an important and actively debated distinction in how these systems are designed and evaluated.

✕

Collaborative filtering requires understanding content. It works purely from behavioral patterns — it can recommend a foreign film with zero metadata as long as enough similar users liked it, no content understanding required.

✓

Correct framing: the "cold start" problem — recommending well when there's little or no history for a new user or item — is a defining challenge of the field, and is exactly why most production systems blend collaborative and content-based signals rather than relying on either alone.
