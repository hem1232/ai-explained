Interpretability

A model with billions of parameters produces an answer — but not an explanation. Interpretability is the field trying to open that box, for reasons ranging from debugging to safety to regulatory compliance.

01 The plain-English version

Most powerful modern models are effectively "black boxes": you can see the input and the output, but the millions or billions of internal numbers that produced that output don't have an obvious, human-readable meaning attached to them.

Interpretability research tries to change that — to find ways of understanding, even approximately, why a model made a specific decision, what concepts it's internally representing, and whether it's reasoning the way you'd want it to.

02 The formal version

Techniques fall broadly into two camps. Post-hoc explanation methods (like LIME or SHAP) don't open the model itself, but instead approximate its local behavior around a specific prediction — for example, by perturbing the input slightly and observing how the output changes, to estimate which features mattered most for that one decision.

Mechanistic interpretability aims deeper: reverse-engineering what individual neurons, attention heads, or groups of them are actually computing, to find human-understandable structure inside the model's weights directly — for instance, identifying a specific attention head that reliably tracks grammatical subject-verb agreement, or a direction in activation space that seems to represent a specific concept.

03 The flow

Two different strategies for the same goal: explain a black box from the outside by testing it, or open it up and try to read its internal computation directly.

04 Worked example

A SHAP explanation, simplified

For a loan denial, a post-hoc method might output feature contributions like: debt_ratio: −0.31 (hurt approval odds), income: +0.12 (helped), credit_history_length: −0.08 (hurt). This tells a loan officer (and, in regulated contexts, the applicant) which specific factors most influenced this specific decision — without needing to understand the model's full internal structure, just its local behavior around this one case.

05 Where this shows up

Real-world use

Interpretability matters for debugging models (finding out a model is relying on a spurious pattern, like a watermark rather than actual image content), regulatory compliance (many jurisdictions require an explanation for automated decisions like loan denials), and increasingly, AI safety — understanding whether a powerful model's internal reasoning matches its stated reasoning is an active, high-priority area of alignment research at major AI labs.

06 Common misconceptions

✕

Interpretability tools give a complete, ground-truth explanation of a model's decision. Most current methods are approximations, and can sometimes give misleading or unstable explanations that don't fully capture the model's actual computation.

✕

If a model can explain its reasoning in words, that explanation is accurate. A model's stated, natural-language explanation for its own output isn't necessarily what actually drove that output internally — this gap between stated and actual reasoning is a specific, active area of interpretability research.

✓

Correct framing: interpretability is an actively evolving field, not a solved problem — current tools provide genuinely useful partial insight, not a complete window into a model's "mind."
