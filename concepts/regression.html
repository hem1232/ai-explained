Linear & Logistic Regression

The simplest useful models in all of ML, and still some of the most widely deployed — because a straight line, drawn well, gets you further than you'd expect.

01 The plain-English version

Linear regression predicts a number by fitting the straight line (or flat plane, in higher dimensions) that best matches the training data — like drawing the best-fit line through a scatterplot of house size vs. price.

Logistic regression, despite the name, predicts a category (usually yes/no) by fitting a curve that outputs a probability between 0 and 1 — "how likely is this email to be spam?" rather than a raw number.

02 The formal version

Linear regression models the relationship as y = w1x1 + w2x2 + ... + b, and finds the weights that minimize the mean squared error between predictions and true values, typically via an exact formula or gradient descent.

Logistic regression applies the same weighted sum, then passes it through the sigmoid function to squash the result into a 0–1 probability, and is trained by minimizing log loss (also called cross-entropy) rather than squared error, since it's optimizing for correct probabilities rather than closeness of a number.

03 The flow

Both algorithms compute the same weighted sum — they differ only in what happens to that sum at the end.

04 Worked example

Predicting loan default risk

A bank models default risk with logistic regression: z = 0.02×(debt_ratio) − 0.01×(income_thousands) + 0.3. For an applicant with debt ratio 40 and income $60k: z = 0.02×40 − 0.01×60 + 0.3 = 0.8 − 0.6 + 0.3 = 0.5. Then sigmoid(0.5) ≈ 0.62 — a 62% estimated probability of default, which the bank can compare against a threshold to approve or deny the loan.

05 Where this shows up

Real-world use

Logistic regression remains extremely common in credit scoring, medical risk prediction, and click-through-rate modeling — industries where regulators require the model's decisions to be explainable, since each weight has a clear, interpretable meaning. Linear regression underlies demand forecasting, pricing models, and countless "predict a number" business problems.

06 Common misconceptions

✕

Logistic regression is a type of regression that predicts numbers, like linear regression. It's actually a classification algorithm — the "regression" in the name refers to how it's fit, not what it outputs.

✕

These are outdated compared to deep learning. On small-to-medium structured/tabular data, regression models often match or beat neural networks, train in seconds, and are far easier to explain to regulators.

✓

Correct framing: these are the right first model to try for most structured-data problems — reach for something more complex only once you've confirmed regression genuinely isn't enough.
