Scaling Laws

One empirical observation, more than any single algorithmic breakthrough, explains why AI labs started building ever-larger models: performance improves in a remarkably predictable way as you scale up.

01 The plain-English version

Scaling laws are empirical patterns showing that a model's performance improves smoothly and predictably as you increase three things together: the number of parameters in the model, the amount of training data, and the amount of compute used to train it.

This predictability is what let AI labs justify enormous training runs before they finished: you can train several smaller models, plot how their performance improves with scale, and extrapolate that curve to estimate how a much larger, much more expensive model will perform — before spending the money to actually build it.

02 The formal version

Research from OpenAI (2020) and later DeepMind's "Chinchilla" paper (2022) found that a model's loss follows an approximate power-law relationship with model size, dataset size, and compute — and critically, that for a fixed compute budget, there's an optimal balance between model size and data size; many earlier large models were significantly undertrained relative to their parameter count, meaning a smaller model trained on proportionally more data would have performed better for the same compute cost.

These laws don't hold indefinitely — they're empirical trends observed over the ranges tested, not fundamental physical laws, and there's active debate over exactly where and how they eventually break down, as well as growing attention to inference-time compute scaling (letting a model "think longer" at answer time) as a complementary lever alongside pretraining scale.

03 The flow

Scaling laws describe how these three inputs jointly and predictably drive down a model's training loss — the empirical basis for planning multi-million-dollar training runs in advance.

04 Worked example

Why Chinchilla changed strategy industry-wide

GPT-3 (2020) had 175 billion parameters but was trained on roughly 300 billion tokens. DeepMind's Chinchilla analysis (2022) found that a compute-optimal model of that same training budget should have used a smaller model with far more training data — their 70-billion-parameter Chinchilla model, trained on 1.4 trillion tokens, outperformed the much larger GPT-3 on most benchmarks. This single finding shifted industry strategy away from "bigger is always better" toward carefully balancing model size against data volume for a given compute budget.

05 Where this shows up

Real-world use

Scaling laws directly inform the multi-hundred-million-dollar decisions AI labs make about how to allocate compute budgets between model size, data collection, and training duration before starting an expensive training run — de-risking these decisions using smaller, cheaper experiments that reliably predict how the full-scale run will behave.

06 Common misconceptions

✕

Scaling laws guarantee AI capabilities will keep improving indefinitely just by adding more compute. They describe smooth, predictable improvement in loss on the training objective — how that translates into specific real-world capabilities, and whether the trend continues past current scales, are both genuinely open questions.

✕

"Bigger model" and "better model" are the same thing. The Chinchilla finding specifically demonstrated the opposite — a smaller, better-trained model can outperform a larger, undertrained one for the same total compute cost.

✓

Correct framing: scaling laws are a powerful predictive tool for planning training runs, not a guarantee about the future — they describe a trend observed so far, within a specific measured range.
