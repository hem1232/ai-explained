Diffusion Models, Step by Step

The dominant technique behind AI image generation works almost backward from how you'd expect: it learns to destroy images with noise, then runs that process in reverse to create new ones.

01 The plain-English version

Training a diffusion model starts by taking real images and gradually adding random noise to them over many steps, until they become pure static. The model's job during training is to learn to reverse each individual step — predict what the slightly-less-noisy version looked like.

To generate a new image, you start from pure random noise and repeatedly ask the trained model to remove a little bit of noise, step by step — after enough steps, coherent structure emerges, and text prompts guide that process toward a specific subject at each step.

02 The formal version

The forward process adds Gaussian noise to an image over T timesteps according to a fixed schedule, until the image is statistically indistinguishable from pure noise. The model is trained to predict the noise that was added at each step, given the noisy image and the timestep — equivalent to learning the reverse process, p(x_{t-1} | x_t).

Generation runs this reverse process from pure noise, iteratively denoising over many steps. Text conditioning (as in text-to-image models) injects the prompt's embedding into the denoising network at each step — typically via cross-attention, similar to how a Transformer's attention mechanism relates different pieces of information — steering the emerging image toward matching the text description.

03 The flow

Training teaches the model to undo noise one step at a time; generation runs that learned reversal starting from pure random noise.

04 Worked example

What one denoising step does

At generation step 40 of 50, the image is still mostly blurry static with faint suggestions of shape. The model looks at this noisy image plus the text prompt "a red fox in snow," and predicts what noise to subtract to move one step closer to a clean image — perhaps sharpening a vague reddish blob into something more clearly fox-shaped. Repeat this prediction-and-subtraction 10 more times, and the final result is a coherent image; each individual step only makes a small, local improvement.

05 Where this shows up

Real-world use

Diffusion models are the technique behind Stable Diffusion, Midjourney, and DALL·E for images, and increasingly power video generation (like Sora) and audio synthesis as well — largely displacing GANs as the dominant generative approach because they tend to produce more stable training and higher-fidelity, more diverse outputs.

06 Common misconceptions

✕

The model "paints" an image the way a human artist would, stroke by stroke. It's refining an entire image's worth of noise simultaneously at each step — more like a photo slowly coming into focus than a canvas being painted left to right.

✕

More denoising steps always means a better image. Past a certain point, additional steps give diminishing returns; modern techniques (like distillation) can produce comparable quality in far fewer steps, which is why some tools can generate images in seconds rather than minutes.

✓

Correct framing: a diffusion model doesn't "know" what a fox looks like in the way a database does — it's learned a general-purpose noise-removal function, and steering it with a text prompt shapes what that removal converges toward.
