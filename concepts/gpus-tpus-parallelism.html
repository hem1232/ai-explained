GPUs, TPUs & Parallel Training

Behind every headline model is an unglamorous but critical fact: training it required arranging thousands of chips to work together without wasting a huge fraction of their time waiting on each other.

01 The plain-English version

Training a neural network is, at its core, an enormous number of matrix multiplications — the same simple operation, repeated billions of times. GPUs (and Google's custom TPUs) are built to do exactly this kind of operation in parallel across thousands of small cores simultaneously, instead of one operation at a time like a general-purpose CPU.

A single chip, no matter how fast, isn't enough to train the largest modern models in a reasonable amount of time — so training gets split across many chips working together, coordinating constantly to stay in sync.

02 The formal version

GPUs are optimized for SIMD (single instruction, multiple data) throughput — applying the same operation across huge arrays of numbers at once — which maps almost perfectly onto matrix multiplication, the core operation in both the forward and backward pass of a neural network. TPUs push this further with hardware specifically designed around the matrix-multiply-and-accumulate operations that dominate deep learning workloads.

Large-scale training uses several forms of parallelism together: data parallelism splits a batch of training examples across chips, each computing gradients on its slice before averaging them; model parallelism splits the model itself across chips when it's too large to fit on one; and pipeline parallelism splits the model into stages, with different chips handling different layers, passing activations along a pipeline to keep every chip busy.

03 The flow

In data parallelism, every GPU trains on a different slice of the batch, then their results are combined so every copy of the model stays identical.

04 Worked example

Why interconnect speed matters as much as chip speed

Training a large model across 1,000 GPUs requires synchronizing gradients between all of them after every single training step. If the network connecting the GPUs is slow, chips spend more time waiting for data to sync than actually computing — this is why frontier AI labs invest as heavily in high-speed interconnects (like NVIDIA's NVLink or InfiniBand networking) as they do in the chips themselves; a cluster of the fastest chips in the world, poorly connected, can train slower than a smaller, well-connected one.

05 Where this shows up

Real-world use

This infrastructure layer is largely invisible to end users but determines what's economically possible in AI: it's why training frontier models is concentrated among a small number of well-funded organizations with access to large GPU clusters, and why cloud providers now compete heavily on offering GPU/TPU compute as a core product.

06 Common misconceptions

✕

More GPUs always means proportionally faster training. Coordination overhead means doubling the chip count rarely doubles training speed — beyond a certain cluster size, returns diminish due to communication bottlenecks.

✕

GPUs are only useful for training, not for running (inference) trained models. GPUs also accelerate inference, especially for large models — though inference has different optimization priorities (latency, cost-per-request) than training does (total throughput).

✓

Correct framing: at the scale of modern AI training, the constraint often isn't raw chip speed but how efficiently thousands of chips can be kept busy and synchronized — a systems engineering problem as much as a hardware one.
