Evaluation: Precision, Recall, F1 & ROC

"95% accurate" can describe both an excellent model and a nearly useless one — it depends entirely on what mistakes cost you.

01 The plain-English version

Precision answers: of everything the model flagged as positive, how much was actually positive? Recall answers: of everything that was actually positive, how much did the model catch? These two usually trade off against each other.

The F1 score combines precision and recall into a single number when you need to balance both. The ROC curve shows how that trade-off shifts as you adjust the model's decision threshold, letting you pick the balance that fits your situation.

02 The formal version

Given a confusion matrix of true/false positives and negatives: Precision = TP / (TP + FP), Recall = TP / (TP + FN), and F1 = 2 × (Precision × Recall) / (Precision + Recall) — the harmonic mean, which punishes a big imbalance between the two more than a simple average would.

The ROC curve plots the true positive rate against the false positive rate across every possible classification threshold; the AUC (area under the curve) summarizes overall discriminative ability in one number, where 1.0 is a perfect classifier and 0.5 is no better than random guessing.

03 The flow

Every metric downstream of the confusion matrix depends on where you set the decision threshold — move it, and precision/recall trade off against each other.

04 Worked example

Why accuracy alone lies

A cancer-screening model is tested on 1,000 patients, only 10 of whom actually have the disease. A lazy model that predicts "no cancer" for everyone scores 99% accuracy — while catching zero real cases. Its recall is 0%. A genuinely useful model here needs high recall (catch nearly every real case) even at the cost of some precision (a few unnecessary follow-up tests) — which is exactly the kind of trade-off accuracy alone hides.

05 Where this shows up

Real-world use

Which metric to optimize depends entirely on the cost of each error type: spam filters favor precision (don't lose real emails to the spam folder), medical screening favors recall (don't miss a real diagnosis), and fraud detection typically needs a carefully tuned balance of both, chosen by looking at the ROC curve at multiple threshold points.

06 Common misconceptions

✕

The model with the highest accuracy is the best model. On imbalanced data — which describes most real-world classification problems — accuracy is often the least informative metric available.

✕

Precision and recall can both be maximized at once. They fundamentally trade off against each other as you move the classification threshold; the goal is choosing the right point on that trade-off, not eliminating it.

✓

Correct framing: decide what a false positive costs you and what a false negative costs you before picking a metric — the "right" metric is a business decision, not a purely mathematical one.
