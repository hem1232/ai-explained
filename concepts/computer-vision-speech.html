Computer Vision & Speech

Two of the earliest and most mature applied AI fields, both built on the same core idea: converting a raw physical signal — light or sound — into something a neural network can reason about.

01 The plain-English version

Computer vision covers tasks like classifying what's in an image, detecting and locating multiple objects within it, and segmenting an image pixel-by-pixel into different regions — built primarily on CNNs and, increasingly, vision Transformers.

Speech AI covers converting spoken audio into text (speech recognition) and converting text into natural-sounding spoken audio (text-to-speech), both of which first convert the raw audio waveform into a spectrogram — a visual representation of sound over time — that a neural network can process similarly to an image.

02 The formal version

Object detection models (like the YOLO family) predict both a class label and a bounding box for every object in an image in a single pass, trading some accuracy for real-time speed; more accurate two-stage detectors first propose candidate regions, then classify each one. Semantic segmentation assigns a class label to every individual pixel, useful where exact boundaries matter, like in medical imaging or autonomous driving.

Modern speech recognition systems typically convert raw audio into a spectrogram via a Fourier transform, then process it with a neural network (often Transformer-based, like Whisper) trained to output the corresponding text transcript — effectively treating speech recognition as a sequence-to-sequence translation problem, from audio-token sequences to text-token sequences.

03 The flow

Both vision and speech systems follow the same broad shape: convert a raw physical signal into a structured representation, then let a trained model extract meaning from it.

04 Worked example

An object detector's raw output

Feeding a street photo through an object detector might return: {class: "car", confidence: 0.94, box: [120,80,340,210]}, {class: "pedestrian", confidence: 0.88, box: [400,60,460,300]} — a list of every object found, each with a confidence score and a bounding box in pixel coordinates, which downstream systems (like a self-driving car's planning module) then use to make decisions.

05 Where this shows up

Real-world use

This category covers an enormous swath of deployed AI: photo organization and search, retail self-checkout, manufacturing quality inspection, medical imaging diagnosis, autonomous vehicle perception, voice assistants, real-time captioning, and call-center transcription and analysis — largely mature, well-understood technology compared to the faster-moving frontier of generative AI.

06 Common misconceptions

✕

Modern computer vision systems are essentially solved and error-free. They remain sensitive to lighting, occlusion, unusual angles, and distribution shift from training data — a detector trained mostly on daytime photos can degrade significantly at night.

✕

Speech recognition just needs to match sounds to words. Accurate transcription also depends heavily on language modeling to resolve ambiguity — "recognize speech" and "wreck a nice beach" sound nearly identical, and context is what disambiguates them.

✓

Correct framing: these systems are highly capable in-distribution — on data similar to what they were trained on — but performance can degrade sharply outside that distribution, which is why real deployments need continuous monitoring, not just a one-time accuracy benchmark.
