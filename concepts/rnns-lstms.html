RNNs & LSTMs for Sequences

Before Transformers, this was how machines read text and time series — one step at a time, carrying a memory forward. Understanding why this approach struggled is what makes attention click.

01 The plain-English version

A Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) processes a sequence one element at a time — one word, one time-step — while carrying forward a "hidden state" that acts like a running summary of everything seen so far.

The problem: over long sequences, that running summary tends to forget early information (the vanishing gradient problem) or lose stability. An LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory) network adds gates that explicitly control what to remember, what to forget, and what to output at each step — giving it a much longer effective memory.

02 The formal version

At each timestep t, a basic RNN computes h_t = activation(W_x · x_t + W_h · h_{t-1} + b) — the new hidden state depends on both the current input and the previous hidden state, which is what makes it "recurrent." Backpropagation through a long sequence (called backpropagation through time) tends to shrink or explode gradients as they're multiplied repeatedly across many timesteps.

An LSTM cell adds a separate cell state that acts as a conveyor belt for long-term information, regulated by a forget gate (what to discard), an input gate (what new information to add), and an output gate (what to expose to the next layer) — each a small learned neural network of its own.

03 The flow

Each step's hidden state depends on the current input plus everything carried forward from before — information flows strictly left to right, one token at a time.

04 Worked example

Where the memory breaks down

In the sentence "The book that the professor, who had taught at three universities over a long and distinguished career, wrote last year became a bestseller," a basic RNN trying to predict "wrote" needs to remember "book" from many words earlier — but by that point, the hidden state has been overwritten dozens of times and the connection has likely faded. An LSTM's dedicated cell state is specifically designed to preserve exactly this kind of long-range dependency better than a plain RNN can.

05 Where this shows up

Real-world use

RNNs and LSTMs were the standard architecture for machine translation, speech recognition, and text generation from roughly 2014–2018, and are still used today in latency-sensitive or resource-constrained settings (like on-device speech processing) where a full Transformer would be overkill. Transformers have since replaced them for most large-scale language tasks specifically because self-attention handles long-range dependencies more directly, without relying on information surviving a long chain of sequential updates.

06 Common misconceptions

✕

LSTMs are obsolete and never used anymore. They remain practical for smaller-scale or real-time sequence tasks where a Transformer's compute and memory cost isn't justified.

✕

RNNs process a whole sequence at once, like Transformers do. RNNs are inherently sequential — step t can't be computed until step t-1 finishes, which also makes them slower to train on modern parallel hardware than Transformers.

✓

Correct framing: RNNs/LSTMs solve sequence modeling through a chain of memory; Transformers solve it through direct attention between all positions at once — the shift from one to the other is one of the most consequential architecture changes in AI history.
