GANs & VAEs

Before diffusion models took over, these two architectures defined the field of AI-generated content — and the core ideas behind both still show up throughout modern generative systems.

01 The plain-English version

A GAN (Generative Adversarial Network) trains two neural networks against each other: a generator that tries to create convincing fake images, and a discriminator that tries to tell real images from the generator's fakes. Each gets better by trying to outdo the other, like a forger and an art authenticator locked in an escalating rivalry.

A VAE (Variational Autoencoder) takes a different approach: it learns to compress data into a compact representation and then reconstruct it, and by structuring that compressed space carefully, you can sample new points from it to generate entirely new, plausible outputs.

02 The formal version

The GAN generator maps random noise to a synthetic sample; the discriminator outputs a probability that a given sample is real. Training alternates: the discriminator is updated to better distinguish real from fake, then the generator is updated to better fool the current discriminator — formally a minimax game that, at convergence, ideally produces a generator whose outputs are indistinguishable from real data.

A VAE consists of an encoder that maps input data to a probability distribution over a compact latent space, and a decoder that reconstructs data from a sampled point in that space. It's trained to minimize both reconstruction error and a regularization term that keeps the latent space smooth and well-structured (via the KL divergence to a standard normal distribution) — that structure is what makes sampling new, coherent outputs possible.

03 The flow

GANs pit two networks against each other in competition; VAEs compress and reconstruct through a structured latent space — different mechanisms, both aimed at generating new data.

04 Worked example

Why GAN training is notoriously tricky

If the discriminator becomes too good too fast, it can distinguish real from fake with near-perfect confidence, giving the generator almost no useful gradient signal to improve from — like being told "wrong" with no hint of how wrong. This is mode collapse's cousin problem, and why GAN training historically required careful balancing between the two networks' learning rates and architectures, unlike diffusion models' comparatively more stable single-network training.

05 Where this shows up

Real-world use

GANs were behind early deepfakes, StyleGAN's photorealistic face generation, and many image super-resolution and style-transfer tools. VAEs are used for anomaly detection (data that reconstructs poorly is likely unusual), and their latent-space concept is directly reused inside modern diffusion models — most text-to-image diffusion actually runs the noising/denoising process in a VAE's compressed latent space rather than on raw pixels, for efficiency.

06 Common misconceptions

✕

GANs and VAEs are now completely obsolete. Diffusion models dominate headline image generation, but GANs remain competitive for fast, real-time generation, and VAE-style latent spaces are a core component inside most modern diffusion pipelines.

✕

The discriminator in a GAN is used at generation time. Once training finishes, only the generator is used to produce new images — the discriminator's entire job was to make the generator better during training.

✓

Correct framing: these two architectures represent two different generative philosophies — adversarial competition (GANs) versus structured compression (VAEs) — and modern systems often borrow ideas from both alongside diffusion.
